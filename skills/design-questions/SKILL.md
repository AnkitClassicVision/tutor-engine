# Design the questions

Use this step after a lesson plan exists. Homework and assessment practice the same topics. They are not the same questions.

## What you read

- The topic title and the one sentence the student should be able to say.
- The job: homework or assessment.
- The agenda from the last checks: what is shaky, what kind of miss keeps happening, and what is already solid.
- Every question this student has already seen on this topic. Those sentences are used up.

## The two moves

Choose two fresh moves at an appropriate demand for this learner; creation is an optional further challenge. A set may be similar to an older set. A sentence may not repeat.

- Application. Put the idea in a real case the student has not been given. They say what happens and why.
- Further challenge when supported: creation. They make a new example, a new question, or a way to grade the idea. They also say what a weak answer would miss.

## Aim at the gap

- Not started or shaky: one item can ask them to say the idea back, and one item shows the weak attempt next to the solid one.
- The same miss twice: point the question at that miss. A slip in the steps is not the same item as a missing idea.
- Solid: do not drill the label again. Homework creates a new example. Assessment uses a new setting.
- A chart, table, map, or graph in the source: one application item makes them read it.

## Homework and assessment

- Homework is practice on the gap. It can coach. The rubric still says what counts.
- Curriculum question records may later be used for assessment: keep these prompts unaided. Put worked examples and faded support in the lesson/example field; do not supply intermediate calculations in stored assessment prompts.
- Assessment is a check. New setting. No hint in the question. It must not reuse a homework sentence.
- A later homework and a later assessment may feel related. Change the case, the ask, or both.

## The rubric

- Name the idea that must be in the answer.
- A title alone does not count.
- Say whether the solid answer applies the idea, creates something, or shows the missing piece and the solid version.
- The question must not contain the answer.
- No student name.
- Do not copy a school-test item. Write a new one on the same idea.

Use brief prerequisite probes, self-explanation, faded examples, delayed retrieval and transfer where the evidence calls for them. Supported teaching and independent assessment stay separate. Do not infer the cause of an error from length or timing alone.

## Clear asks and useful variety

State exactly whether the learner should name a rule, list steps, explain, calculate, or check reasoning. A steps-only probe must explicitly say not to solve yet and its rubric must not require a numeric result. Do not grade an unstated requirement. Accept equivalent words for the same valid procedure.

Vary the representation, situation and requested thinking where appropriate, rather than only changing numbers. Use learner-question clarification signals within the offered concept scope, as requests rather than mistakes. Keep one small goal per task and enough information to answer it. Preserve due reviews and strong topics alongside gaps; a support request does not justify lowering every topic. Match the actual Bloom demand to what the learner must do.

Read approved topic keys, dimensions, success criteria, contrasts and misconception fixes when supplied. Bind to the explicit key, never a similarly named topic. Factual knowledge calls for meaningful recall, conceptual knowledge for distinguishing and explaining cases, procedural knowledge for applying steps with reasons, and metacognitive knowledge for choosing and checking strategies. Preserve the requested demand and rubric. A known contrast is teaching material, not a fresh independent item: use a different case, not a paraphrase or equivalent calculation.
